# Concept: a federal pay-equity policy organization

## Who it's for

All working women in the U.S. — matching the scope of the Pew data this
concept is built on. [Pew — U.S. gender pay gap](../sources/us-gender-pay-gap-pew.md)

## What it is

A **policy advocacy nonprofit** — not a program, not a direct service
provider. It doesn't deliver anything to individual women; it exists to
change federal law.

## What it does

Three activities, all aimed at one federal legislative package:

1. **Research** — translates data like Pew's into concrete policy
   proposals, one per identified cause of the gap.
2. **Coalition-building** — recruits other nonprofits, unions, and
   companies to publicly co-sign the package, adding political weight
   without the org needing lobbyists of its own.
3. **Public campaign** — media, social, and grassroots pressure aimed at
   voters and press, building the public support that makes the ask harder
   to ignore.

## The ask: one package, three parts

Rather than picking a single flagship bill, the package addresses all three
causes documented in [the problem statement](problem-statement.md):

- **Caregiving support** (addressing reason #1 — reduced hours during
  caregiving years, and the unmatched "fatherhood wage premium")
- **Incentives into higher-paying fields** (addressing reason #2 —
  occupational segregation into lower-paying, female-dominated occupations)
- **Pay transparency / disaggregated reporting requirements** (addressing
  reason #3 — discrimination that Pew itself says is hard to quantify
  without better data; this mirrors the transparency reform Korea is
  currently debating, though that source describes Korea's policy debate,
  not a U.S. precedent — [Korea Times](../sources/korea-gender-pay-gap.md))

## How it works in the real world

Federal level only. The org has no direct lobbyists in congressional
offices — it builds the case (research), builds the backers (coalition),
and builds the pressure (public campaign), and lets its coalition partners
and public pressure do the direct legislative engagement.

## What this deliberately leaves out

- **Direct lobbying** — no congressional meetings or testimony run by the
  org itself.
- **State-level policy work** — federal only, even though several states
  already have pay transparency laws; this org doesn't pursue state
  legislatures, even as a proof-of-concept path to a federal win.
- **International/comparative policy work** — Korea's transparency-reform
  debate and the UK/France/Germany/Norway laws it cites are background
  context for the U.S. ask, not something this org acts on directly.
- **Direct services to women** — no job training, legal aid, or other
  service delivery. Pure policy and advocacy.

## Open gap

No source in this wiki measures whether any of these three federal policies
— caregiving support, occupational-segregation incentives, or pay
transparency — actually moves the pay gap in the U.S. specifically. The
case for "this would work" currently rests on Pew's diagnosis of the causes,
not on evidence that these particular interventions fix them.

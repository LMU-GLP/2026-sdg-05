# Policy report: complementary solutions for each cause

This report examines existing US policy — past and present — against each of
the three structural causes named in [Root causes](root-causes.md), then
proposes a complement for each. The policy sections below are all cited to
`sources/`. The **proposed complement** under each cause is this team's own
reasoning, not a sourced claim — flagged clearly as such.

## Cause 1: Occupational segregation

### Past & present policy

The Equal Pay Act (1963) and Title IX (1972) are the foundational laws, and
the US Department of Labor's own Women's Bureau names this cause directly:
"Too often, women and people of color are shut out of high-paying
opportunities... This kind of occupational segregation is one of the
biggest barriers to economic equity for women," and separately, "the single
largest cause of wage gaps are differences in the jobs people hold."
[DOL Women's Bureau](../sources/dol-womens-bureau-occupational-segregation.md)

### The gap

No source in this wiki describes a running federal program with reach or
outcome figures that targets occupational segregation directly — unlike
caregiving and workplace inflexibility (below), which have specific laws
attached, this cause currently has a diagnosis (from DOL itself) but no
program in this wiki's evidence.

### Proposed complement (team's own reasoning, not a cited fact)

Since AAUW's Work Smart (individual negotiation skills) and state pay
transparency laws (information at time of hire) don't change which fields
women enter in the first place, a complement aimed at this cause would need
to work further upstream — before a pay negotiation ever happens — for
example, funding or incentives that connect girls and young women to
higher-paying nontraditional fields (skilled trades, STEM) the way Title IX
already opened career and technical education access. This wiki has no
evidence yet on which specific program design would work; it's a direction,
not a plan.

## Cause 2: Caregiving / the motherhood penalty

### Past & present policy

The **FMLA (1993)** guarantees "up to 12 workweeks" of **unpaid**,
job-protected leave, but only for employers with 50+ employees, and only for
employees meeting hours-worked and tenure thresholds.
[DOL: FMLA](../sources/dol-fmla-basics.md) Historically, this excluded a
large share of the workforce: as of 2003 only ~60% of private-sector
workers were covered by the employer-size rule alone, and adding the
hours-worked rule dropped eligibility to 46%; in 2007, of 141.7 million US
workers, 65.6 million did not meet eligibility criteria at all.
[FMLA coverage gaps](../sources/fmla-1993-coverage-gaps.md)

Since then, **24 states plus DC** have added their own paid family leave
laws (14 mandatory, 10 voluntary), starting with California in 2004 and most
recently Virginia (2026).
[State paid leave laws](../sources/bipartisan-policy-state-paid-leave-2026.md)
A federal bill, the **More Paid Leave for More Americans Act**, was
introduced 2026-07-16 — but it funds and coordinates *state* programs via
grants, rather than creating a national paid-leave entitlement.
[More Paid Leave for More Americans Act](../sources/bipartisan-policy-more-paid-leave-act-2026.md)

### The gap

Roughly three-quarters of states still have no mandatory paid leave law, 32
years after FMLA passed unpaid leave into federal law, and the 2026 federal
bill is an incentive/grant structure, not a mandate.

### Proposed complement (team's own reasoning, not a cited fact)

A complement here doesn't need to invent a new mechanism — the More Paid
Leave for More Americans Act's own model (state grants) already targets the
gap. What's missing in this wiki's evidence is anything aimed at the states
that have neither a mandatory nor voluntary program at all — pairing the
federal grant approach with direct advocacy in those specific states would
close the coverage gap faster than waiting for all 50 states to act
independently. This is a direction the wiki's evidence supports, not a
sourced program.

## Cause 3: Workplace inflexibility

### Past & present policy

Two federal laws address narrow slices of this cause. The **PUMP Act**
(effective 2022-12-29) guarantees "reasonable break time" and private space
to pump breast milk for one year after birth, extended to previously
excluded workers like agricultural, transportation, and home-care workers.
[DOL: PUMP Act](../sources/dol-pump-act.md) The **Pregnant Workers Fairness
Act** (effective 2023-06-27) is broader: it guarantees schedule changes,
breaks, and even remote work as accommodations — but only during and
shortly after pregnancy, not for ongoing caregiving once a child is past
infancy. [PWFA](../sources/pwfa-pregnant-workers-fairness-act.md)

The only *general*, ongoing right-to-request-flexibility law in this wiki is
local, not federal or even statewide: San Francisco's Family Friendly
Workplace Ordinance (updated 2022), covering employees of SF-based employers
with 20+ workers, letting any caregiver — not just new parents — request an
alternative schedule.
[SF Family Friendly Workplace Ordinance](../sources/sf-family-friendly-workplace-ordinance.md)

### The gap

Federal law only guarantees flexibility around pregnancy and infant feeding.
Once a child (or another dependent) is older, no federal law in this wiki
guarantees a worker the right to even *request* schedule flexibility tied to
ongoing caregiving — the only such right found is a single city's ordinance.

### Proposed complement (team's own reasoning, not a cited fact)

The PWFA already proves a right-to-request-flexibility model can pass at the
federal level for pregnancy; San Francisco's ordinance proves the same model
can extend to caregiving broadly. Combining the two — a federal
right-to-request law modeled on the SF ordinance's caregiver-status test,
rather than PWFA's pregnancy-status test — is a direction the two existing
laws in this wiki suggest, but no source describes such a bill existing or
being proposed federally.

## Summary table

| Cause | Strongest existing policy | Its limit | Proposed complement direction |
|---|---|---|---|
| Occupational segregation | Title IX (education access) | No program targets the *labor market* outcome, only education | Upstream field-entry incentives, not yet designed |
| Caregiving / motherhood penalty | State paid leave (24 states+DC) + 2026 federal grant bill | ~26 states still have no mandatory law; federal bill funds states, doesn't mandate | Pair federal grants with targeted advocacy in zero-coverage states |
| Workplace inflexibility | PWFA (federal, pregnancy-linked) | Doesn't cover ongoing caregiving past infancy | Federal right-to-request law modeled on SF's caregiver-status test |

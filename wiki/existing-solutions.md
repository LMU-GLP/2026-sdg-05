# Existing solutions

Two things are already running today, and one federal fix is stalled.

## AAUW Work Smart — individual-skills program (running)

A salary-negotiation training program that has trained **190,000+ women** to
date. **70%** of participants report it had a positive impact on their
ability to negotiate a fair salary. The program's own materials cite the
pay gap at 83 cents on the dollar and warn that, without intervention, "the
pay gap will not close til 2106."
[AAUW Work Smart](../sources/aauw-work-smart-program.md)

This is an individual-level intervention: it helps women negotiate better
within the system as it exists, and doesn't change employer pay practices or
law.

## State pay transparency laws — policy, but patchwork (running)

**14 states** (California, Colorado, Connecticut, Hawaii, Illinois, Maryland,
Massachusetts, Minnesota, Nevada, New Jersey, New York, Rhode Island,
Vermont, Washington) require employers to disclose salary ranges in job
postings as of 2026. Coverage thresholds vary by state (4 to 50+ employees).
[Pay transparency laws](../sources/pay-transparency-laws-2026.md)

This is real, enforced policy — but only in 14 states. Most of the country,
and any employer below a state's employee threshold, has no such requirement.

## Paycheck Fairness Act — federal policy (stalled)

Would close loopholes in the Equal Pay Act of 1963: ban using salary history
to set pay, protect workers from retaliation for discussing pay, require
wage-data collection, and support negotiation-training programs. First
introduced in this form in January 2019, failed a Senate procedural vote in
June 2021, and as of a 2026 industry summary still has not passed at the
federal level.
[AAUW: Paycheck Fairness Act](../sources/aauw-paycheck-fairness-act.md)

## What this means for a new solution

The individual-skills approach (Work Smart) and the state-policy approach
(transparency laws) are both real and both partial — neither reaches
everyone, and the one solution that would (federal law) has failed to pass
for at least seven years. A new solution has room to work in either lane:
help more individuals directly, or push transparency into more states/employers,
rather than waiting on federal action.

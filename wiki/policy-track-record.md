# US policy track record: what's been tried, what it did, why the gap remains

This page exists to answer one question directly: **which US policies aimed at
the gender pay gap have actually been implemented, what measurable effect did
each have, and why hasn't any of them closed the gap?** It also proposes new
directions grounded in what the evidence says is actually driving the
remaining gap, rather than restating "reduce bias" as a plan.

## 1. Policies actually implemented in the US

| Policy | Level | Year | What it does |
|---|---|---|---|
| Equal Pay Act | Federal | 1963 | Bans paying men and women differently for equal work |
| Title VII, Civil Rights Act | Federal | 1964 | Bans employment discrimination on the basis of sex, more broadly than pay |
| Lilly Ledbetter Fair Pay Act | Federal | 2009 | Resets the 180-day statute of limitations on pay-discrimination lawsuits with every discriminatory paycheck, not just the original pay decision |
| Family and Medical Leave Act (FMLA) | Federal | 1993 | 12 weeks of unpaid, job-protected leave for birth/family/medical reasons |
| Paid Family Leave | State (CA first, now ~13 states) | 2004– | *Paid* leave, funded through payroll tax, unlike FMLA |
| Salary history bans | State/local (~half of states) | 2017– | Bars employers from asking applicants what they previously earned |
| Pay transparency laws | State (14 states) | ongoing | Requires posting salary ranges in job listings — [see existing sources](../sources/pay-transparency-laws-2026.md) |
| Paycheck Fairness Act | Federal (proposed, not passed) | introduced 2019, failed Senate vote 2021 | Would ban salary-history use nationwide, protect pay-discussion rights, require wage-data reporting — [see existing source](../sources/aauw-paycheck-fairness-act.md) |

Sources: [EEOC — Lilly Ledbetter Fair Pay Act](../sources/eeoc-lilly-ledbetter-fair-pay-act-2009.md),
[American Progress — Equal Pay Act at 60](../sources/americanprogress-equal-pay-act-turns-60.md),
[MIT Sloan — FMLA](../sources/mitsloan-fmla-womens-careers.md),
[Equitable Growth — CA Paid Family Leave](../sources/equitablegrowth-ca-paid-family-leave-2004.md),
[HR Dive — salary history bans](../sources/hrdive-salary-history-ban-2025.md),
[NBER — CA salary history ban](../sources/nber-california-salary-history-ban.md).

## 2. Measured impact of each

- **Equal Pay Act (1963):** the full-time gap narrowed from **59 cents** on the
  dollar in 1963 to **84 cents** by 2021. Real progress, but at that pace,
  American Progress projects parity for full-time women only by **2056**.
  [American Progress](../sources/americanprogress-equal-pay-act-turns-60.md)
- **Lilly Ledbetter Fair Pay Act (2009):** doesn't change how much anyone is
  paid — it's a procedural fix, making the 1963/1964 protections usable when
  discrimination is discovered years after the original pay decision.
  [EEOC](../sources/eeoc-lilly-ledbetter-fair-pay-act-2009.md)
- **FMLA (1993) — mixed, and contested:** one study of 785 workplaces found
  women's representation in management rose steadily *after* FMLA passed but
  not before. [MIT Sloan](../sources/mitsloan-fmla-womens-careers.md) A
  separate line of research finds the opposite effect on pay specifically:
  gender wage convergence slowed by roughly **76%**
  ([TIME/Blair](../sources/time-blair-family-leave-wage-convergence.md)), or
  from about "a percentage point a year" before to "about a third of a point"
  after ([IFS](../sources/ifstudies-parental-leave-wage-convergence.md)). These
  measure different outcomes (management representation vs. wage
  convergence) — both are kept here rather than treated as contradicting.
- **State Paid Family Leave (CA, since 2004):** mothers with access saw an
  "approximately 20 percent increase" in labor-force participation in their
  child's birth year, an effect still measurable 5 years out — but the gains
  were "more pronounced and longer lasting for white, highly educated women,"
  with much smaller effects for lower-income families under California's
  original 55%-wage-replacement design. [Equitable Growth](../sources/equitablegrowth-ca-paid-family-leave-2004.md)
- **Salary history bans:** one Boston University-cited study found female
  new-hire salaries rose **7.8 percentage points** more in ban states than
  non-ban states; a separate NBER study of California alone found a smaller,
  **1 percentage point** rise in the gender earnings ratio, concentrated among
  job-switchers. Different sizes, same direction: bans help, modestly, and
  mainly for people changing jobs. [HR Dive](../sources/hrdive-salary-history-ban-2025.md),
  [NBER](../sources/nber-california-salary-history-ban.md)
- **Pay transparency laws and the Paycheck Fairness Act:** already covered in
  [Existing solutions](existing-solutions.md) — transparency laws are real but
  patchwork (14 states); the federal bill that would apply everywhere has
  failed to pass since at least 2019.

## 3. Why the gap persists despite all of this

Two different mechanisms show up across these sources, and neither is fixed
by banning direct discrimination:

- **"Greedy work."** Claudia Goldin's research finds that large pay
  differences appear "about 10 years after college graduation," timed to when
  children arrive, because jobs that pay a premium for long, inflexible hours
  reward the parent who *doesn't* take on flexible/caregiving work — and
  couples still specialize, with mothers taking the flexible role. This is
  "the lion's share of the pay gap" beyond direct discrimination or
  occupational segregation, and it's why "the gender pay gap among college
  graduates has remained flat" even as women's credentials improved.
  [Goldin](../sources/goldin-career-and-family-greedy-work.md)
- **Leave-taking asymmetry creates employer statistical discrimination.**
  Because "women take approximately four times more days of leave" than men
  around a birth, employers factor that expected difference into hiring and
  promotion decisions — which is the mechanism researchers point to for why
  family-leave mandates slowed wage convergence rather than closing it.
  [TIME/Blair](../sources/time-blair-family-leave-wage-convergence.md)
- **Structural causes already named in this wiki:** occupational segregation,
  women being "twice as likely as men to work part-time," and "insufficient
  paid leave, affordable childcare, and workplace flexibility."
  [AAUW causes report](../sources/aauw-causes-report-2025.md)

In short: the laws on the books ban *unequal pay for the same work* and
*discrimination in hiring*. The remaining gap is driven by *unequal
caregiving load* translating into unequal hours, unequal leave-taking, and
employer expectations built on that asymmetry — none of which the Equal Pay
Act, Ledbetter Act, or FMLA were designed to touch.

## 4. Where a new solution has room (grounded in the evidence above, not a guess)

These build directly on named gaps in the policies above, rather than
restating "reduce bias" or "pay women more":

1. **Make the second parent's leave non-transferable, not just available.**
   The causal mechanism the evidence points to is take-up asymmetry (women
   take ~4x the leave days), not the mere existence of leave law — so a
   design that changes *who takes leave*, not just whether leave exists,
   targets the actual mechanism. **No US source in this wiki documents a
   non-transferable "use-it-or-lose-it" leave allotment for a second parent
   having been implemented and evaluated in the US** — this is a design
   direction the mechanism evidence supports, not a proven US program, and
   should be flagged as such if proposed.
2. **Take salary-history bans and pay-transparency laws federal.** Both are
   proven (see above) but exist in well under half the states each. A team
   solution could lobby/model federal adoption, or build a tool that gives
   workers in *non*-covered states the transparency those laws would provide.
3. **Target the wage-replacement rate, not just leave's existence.**
   California's own experience shows a 55%-replacement design helped mostly
   already-advantaged women, and gains for lower-income families only grew
   after raising replacement to 70%. Any new paid-leave proposal should treat
   the replacement rate as a design variable, not an afterthought.
   [Equitable Growth](../sources/equitablegrowth-ca-paid-family-leave-2004.md)
4. **Aim at "greedy work" directly, not just at parents.** Goldin's framing
   implies that policies making flexible schedules less penalized in
   high-hourly-premium jobs address the root mechanism more directly than
   further anti-discrimination law — this wiki does not yet have a source
   evaluating any specific US policy that does this, so it's named here as an
   open gap for further research, not a claim.

## What's still missing from this wiki

No source here yet evaluates a *US* non-transferable parental-leave quota (the
"daddy quota" idea) as an implemented-and-measured program — most of the
strong evidence on that specific design comes from other countries' systems,
which this wiki should not cite alongside US figures without flagging that
they measure different populations under different laws (see CLAUDE.md rule 5).
If the team wants that comparison, it needs its own source file, kept
separate from the US figures above.

# US policy track record, organized by cause

This team named three causes of the US gender pay gap: **(1) caregiving and
the motherhood penalty, (2) occupational segregation into lower-paying
fields, and (3) unconscious bias**. This page organizes what's actually been
tried in the US against each cause, using the same four questions every time:
what policy exists, what did it measurably do, why hasn't it closed the gap,
and where does the evidence point for a new solution.

## Cause 1: Caregiving and the motherhood penalty

### 1. Policies implemented

| Policy | Level | Year | What it does |
|---|---|---|---|
| Family and Medical Leave Act (FMLA) | Federal | 1993 | 12 weeks of unpaid, job-protected leave for birth/family/medical reasons |
| Paid Family Leave | State (CA first, now ~13 states) | 2004– | *Paid* leave, funded through payroll tax, unlike FMLA |

### 2. Measured impact

- **FMLA — mixed, and contested.** One study of 785 workplaces found women's
  representation in management rose steadily *after* FMLA passed but not
  before. [MIT Sloan](../sources/mitsloan-fmla-womens-careers.md) A separate
  line of research finds the opposite effect on pay specifically: gender wage
  convergence slowed by roughly **76%** ([TIME/Blair](../sources/time-blair-family-leave-wage-convergence.md)),
  or from about "a percentage point a year" before FMLA to "about a third of a
  point" after ([IFS](../sources/ifstudies-parental-leave-wage-convergence.md)).
  These measure different outcomes (management representation vs. wage
  convergence) — both are kept here rather than treated as contradicting.
- **State Paid Family Leave (CA, since 2004):** mothers with access saw an
  "approximately 20 percent increase" in labor-force participation in their
  child's birth year, an effect still measurable 5 years out — but the gains
  were "more pronounced and longer lasting for white, highly educated women,"
  with much smaller effects for lower-income families under California's
  original 55%-wage-replacement design.
  [Equitable Growth](../sources/equitablegrowth-ca-paid-family-leave-2004.md)

### 3. Why the gap persists anyway

- **"Greedy work."** Claudia Goldin's research finds large pay differences
  appear "about 10 years after college graduation," timed to when children
  arrive, because jobs that pay a premium for long, inflexible hours reward
  the parent who *doesn't* take on flexible/caregiving work — and couples
  still specialize, with mothers taking the flexible role. This is "the
  lion's share of the pay gap" beyond direct discrimination, and it's why
  "the gender pay gap among college graduates has remained flat" even as
  women's credentials improved. [Goldin](../sources/goldin-career-and-family-greedy-work.md)
- **Leave-taking asymmetry creates employer statistical discrimination.**
  Because "women take approximately four times more days of leave" than men
  around a birth, employers factor that expected difference into hiring and
  promotion decisions — the mechanism researchers point to for why
  family-leave mandates slowed wage convergence rather than closing it.
  [TIME/Blair](../sources/time-blair-family-leave-wage-convergence.md)
- **Design, not just existence, of paid leave matters.** California's own
  55%-replacement design helped mostly already-advantaged women; only after
  raising replacement to 70% did lower-income families see comparable gains.
  [Equitable Growth](../sources/equitablegrowth-ca-paid-family-leave-2004.md)

### 4. Where the evidence points for a supplement

- **Make the second parent's leave non-transferable, not just available.**
  The mechanism above is take-up asymmetry (women take ~4x the leave days),
  not the mere existence of leave law — so a design that changes *who* takes
  leave, not just whether leave exists, targets the actual mechanism.
  **No US source in this wiki documents a non-transferable
  "use-it-or-lose-it" leave allotment for a second parent having been
  implemented and evaluated in the US** — this is a design direction the
  mechanism evidence supports, not a proven US program, and must be flagged
  as such if proposed. (Other countries have tried this; their figures
  measure different populations under different laws and should not be
  quoted next to the US figures above without their own source file — see
  CLAUDE.md rule 5.)
- **Treat the wage-replacement rate as a design variable.** Any new paid-leave
  proposal should specify the replacement rate and model who it reaches at
  each level, rather than proposing "paid leave" generically.

## Cause 2: Occupational segregation into lower-paying fields

### 1. Policies implemented

**No source in this wiki documents a US policy implemented specifically to
address occupational segregation** (e.g., moving women into higher-paying,
male-dominated fields). The one source that names this cause,
[AAUW's causes report](../sources/aauw-causes-report-2025.md), describes the
problem but does not name or evaluate a specific policy response.

### 2–4. Impact / why it persists / supplement

Not answerable from sourced material yet. Per this wiki's rule against
supplying unsourced figures, this section stays a flagged gap rather than a
guess. Candidate US policies that would need their own source files before
they could be written up here: Title IX's effect on women's entry into STEM
and professional fields, the Women in Apprenticeship and Nontraditional
Occupations (WANTO) Act, or federal-contractor affirmative-action rules
(Executive Order 11246). None of these has been verified against a primary
source for this wiki yet — say so rather than filling the gap with a plausible
guess.

## Cause 3: Unconscious bias (in pay-setting)

### 1. Policies implemented

| Policy | Level | Year | What it does |
|---|---|---|---|
| Equal Pay Act | Federal | 1963 | Bans paying men and women differently for equal work |
| Title VII, Civil Rights Act | Federal | 1964 | Bans employment discrimination on the basis of sex, more broadly than pay |
| Lilly Ledbetter Fair Pay Act | Federal | 2009 | Resets the 180-day statute of limitations on pay-discrimination lawsuits with every discriminatory paycheck, not just the original pay decision |
| Salary history bans | State/local (~half of states) | 2017– | Bars employers from asking applicants what they previously earned |
| Pay transparency laws | State (14 states) | ongoing | Requires posting salary ranges in job listings |
| Paycheck Fairness Act | Federal (proposed, not passed) | introduced 2019, failed Senate vote 2021 | Would ban salary-history use nationwide, protect pay-discussion rights, require wage-data reporting |

**Important framing note:** none of these laws targets "unconscious bias" as
a psychological phenomenon (there is no bias-training mandate among them) —
they target the *outcome* bias produces (unequal pay) by banning it or making
it visible. Keep that distinction if proposing a bias-*awareness*
intervention, since it would be a different kind of policy than anything in
this table.

### 2. Measured impact

- **Equal Pay Act (1963):** the full-time gap narrowed from **59 cents** on
  the dollar in 1963 to **84 cents** by 2021. Real progress, but at that
  pace, American Progress projects parity for full-time women only by
  **2056**. [American Progress](../sources/americanprogress-equal-pay-act-turns-60.md)
- **Lilly Ledbetter Fair Pay Act (2009):** doesn't change how much anyone is
  paid — it's a procedural fix, making the 1963/1964 protections usable when
  discrimination is discovered years after the original pay decision.
  [EEOC](../sources/eeoc-lilly-ledbetter-fair-pay-act-2009.md)
- **Salary history bans:** one Boston University-cited study found female
  new-hire salaries rose **7.8 percentage points** more in ban states than
  non-ban states; a separate NBER study of California alone found a smaller,
  **1 percentage point** rise in the gender earnings ratio, concentrated
  among job-switchers. Different sizes, same direction.
  [HR Dive](../sources/hrdive-salary-history-ban-2025.md),
  [NBER](../sources/nber-california-salary-history-ban.md)
- **Pay transparency laws and the Paycheck Fairness Act:** transparency laws
  are real but patchwork (14 states); the federal bill that would apply
  everywhere has failed to pass since at least 2019. See
  [Existing solutions](existing-solutions.md).

### 3. Why the gap persists anyway

- **Enforcement is a patchwork, not a floor.** Salary history bans and pay
  transparency laws each cover well under half the country; the one bill
  that would make either universal (Paycheck Fairness Act) has failed every
  attempt since 2019.
- **The remaining gap isn't mainly about direct pay-setting bias anymore.**
  Goldin's research argues "the lion's share of the pay gap comes from
  something else" beyond direct discrimination — pointing back to Cause 1
  (caregiving/greedy work) as the larger remaining driver once outright
  discrimination is banned. [Goldin](../sources/goldin-career-and-family-greedy-work.md)

### 4. Where the evidence points for a supplement

- **Take salary-history bans and pay-transparency laws federal**, closing the
  state-by-state coverage gap directly, rather than proposing a new
  mechanism.
- **Pass or model the Paycheck Fairness Act's specific provisions** (national
  salary-history ban, pay-discussion protection, wage-data reporting) rather
  than a new bill from scratch — it already specifies what's missing from
  current federal law. [AAUW](../sources/aauw-paycheck-fairness-act.md)
